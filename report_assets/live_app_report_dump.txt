One shared bottleneck

How do TCP and UDP behave as traffic approaches and exceeds the capacity of a bottleneck link, and what changes when both share it?

Two senders connect to Router A over fast links. Router A forwards everything to Router B over a single slow link, then Router B delivers to two receivers. The fast links are twenty times quicker than the bottleneck, so packets can only pile up in one place: the queue at Router A. When that queue is full, new packets are dropped.

TCP waits for acknowledgements, resends lost data and slows down when it sees loss. UDP does none of that: it sends at whatever rate the application chooses. The four experiments below test what that difference means in practice.

Goodput is application data that actually reached the receiver, measured from 1 s to 10 s. Delay and jitter come from NS-3’s FlowMonitor at the IP layer.

Experiment A

Low load: both protocols keep up

Each protocol sends 2 Mbps of 1024-byte packets, paced by the same application, over a 20 Mbps bottleneck. The link has ten times the capacity needed, so this run shows the protocols without congestion.

TCP delivered 1.992 Mbps and UDP 1.997 Mbps. Delay is 12.60 ms and 12.59 ms, close to the 12 ms of propagation along the path plus the time to transmit each packet. The queue never dropped a packet.

Experiment B

Overload: sending faster does not deliver more

One UDP source sends at a fixed rate over the 5 Mbps bottleneck. Three separate runs use 2, 5, 8 Mbps. The rate the source asks for is not the rate that arrives, and the gap between the two is the point of this experiment.

At 2 Mbps everything arrives: 2.00 Mbps delivered, 0.0% loss, 13.9 ms delay. At 8 Mbps the receiver still gets only 4.85 Mbps. The other 38.7% of packets are dropped at Router A, and the ones that survive wait in a full queue, so delay rises to 98 ms.

Delivered goodput levels off just under 5 Mbps because every packet on the wire also carries UDP, IP and link headers. For the same reason, offering exactly 5 Mbps of payload is already a slight overload.

Experiment C

TCP finds the limit, backs off, tries again

A single TCP flow sends as much data as it is allowed to. Nobody tells it the link speed. Instead, TCP keeps growing its congestion window, the amount of data it may have in flight, until a packet is lost. It then cuts the window and starts growing it again.

In the chart you can see slow start shoot the window up in the first few hundred milliseconds, overfill the queue and lose packets. After that NewReno grows the window slowly, by about one packet per round trip, until the queue overflows again.

Experiment D

Sharing the link: TCP yields, UDP does not

Now both senders run at once. TCP sends as fast as it is allowed, and UDP offers a constant 3 Mbps. Both go through the same 5 Mbps link and the same queue.

UDP got 2.96 Mbps through, losing 0.4% of its packets. TCP got 1.85 Mbps, roughly what was left. When the queue overflowed, TCP saw the loss and shrank its window. UDP saw nothing and kept sending at the same rate.

That is what “no congestion control” means in practice: an unresponsive flow keeps its share, and responsive flows around it give way.

The two senders are not equally provisioned, so this is not a fairness measurement. The split depends on the configured UDP rate. Re-run with --set udpRateMbps=4.5 to see it change.

How the numbers were measured

Everything on this page is read from results/summary.csv, timeseries.csv and cwnd.csv, written by the NS-3.49 simulation. A missing run shows up as an empty panel, never as an invented value.

Goodput
payload bytes received between 1 s and 10 s × 8 ÷ 9 s ÷ 10⁶
Mean delay
sum of packet delays ÷ received packets (FlowMonitor)
Mean jitter
sum of delay differences ÷ (received packets − 1)
UDP loss
(packets sent − packets received) ÷ packets sent
Queue drops
counted from the Drop trace of Router A’s FIFO queue
Reproduce it
# in WSL Ubuntu, from the project folder
export NS3_ROOT=~/ns-3.49
python3 scripts/run_experiments.py           # all 7 runs + charts
python3 scripts/run_experiments.py --only mixed --set queuePackets=10

# on Windows, in frontend/
npm run dev                                  # syncs the CSVs, starts the site
Limits
FlowMonitor measures at the IP layer, so TCP delay includes retransmitted packets.
TCP loss is not reported as data lost: TCP resends it. The cost appears as lower goodput and window cuts instead.
A TCP goodput bin can briefly exceed the link rate when held-back data is released after a retransmission.
There is no randomness in the scenario, so every run is exactly repeatable and the seed has no effect.
One flow per protocol, one queue size, DropTail only. The results show mechanisms, not general rules.
